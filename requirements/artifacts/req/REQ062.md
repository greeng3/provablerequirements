What is generated for a checker must be expressible in the language that checker actually reads,
which is not always the language the surrounding artifact is written in. A checker may accept an
ordinary program and yet interpret the interior of an assertion in a restricted logic of its own,
in which constructs that are unremarkable elsewhere are rejected outright — in particular ones
that must be expanded before they can be read.

A tool that generates one artifact for several checkers therefore cannot treat the most permissive
of them as the standard. What it emits belongs to the intersection: the form every intended checker
reads. Where a convenient construct is only shorthand for a plainer one, the plainer one is what is
generated — it costs nothing at the permissive checkers and is the only form the strict one accepts,
so one output continues to serve all of them rather than splitting into a dialect per checker.

Getting this wrong does not produce a wrong answer. It produces an inconclusive verdict carrying
the checker's objection to the generated text, which reaches the operator as a complaint about
their subject when it is a limitation of the tool's own output. The distinction matters because the
operator can act on the first and cannot act on the second.
