A checker may decline a construct of the subject's language outright — not because the claim is
wrong or the subject is malformed, but because translating that construct is beyond what the
checker does. Where the checker says so, the tool reports it as the checker's limit, and names the
construct.

Two things about such a refusal are not obvious from the checker's own message and must be said.

The first is its reach. Where a checker takes in the whole subject rather than only the part a
claim mentions, one untranslatable construct anywhere blocks every claim about that subject,
including claims whose subject matter is nowhere near it. An operator reading a complaint about a
construct their requirement never mentions will otherwise look for the connection, and there is
none to find.

The second is the way out, where one exists. A checker that cannot translate a construct may still
allow the items using it to be declared outside what is verified, and then proceed with the rest.
That is a decision the operator makes about their own subject — it narrows what has been checked,
which is theirs to weigh — so the tool states that the option exists and leaves it there. Advice
that would not help must not be offered in its place: where no specification the operator could
write would make the construct translatable, suggesting one wastes their effort and misdescribes
the obstacle.
